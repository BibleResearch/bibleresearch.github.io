I'm reading more about alternative eschatological views and have noticed a few key differences between how the Dispensational Pre-millennial and Partial Preterist views (which could be either Post-mil. or Amil.) think about the Biblical data on the end times.

# Key Differences

## OT vs. NT Ordering

Pre-millennialism generally starts with the book of Revelation and reads this back onto everything else, whereas Partial Preterism (and other forms of Amillennial and Post-millennial thought) starts with the OT and works into the NT.

If you listen to a Pre-millennialist explain their viewpoint, they will almost always start in the book of Revelation. If you listen to other views explain their thought, they often start in Genesis and work their way through Scripture to Revelation.

One example where this comes into play is Psalm 110. Assuming the request from God the Father in Psalm 110:1 is accepted by God the Son, the chronology given is:

1. Son sits at right hand
2. While Son is sitting at right hand: Enemies are made a footstool

So someone constructing eschatological expectations from Psalm 110 would walk away thinking that God the Father is going to make Christ's enemies a footstool while Christ is sitting at the right hand of God the Father (not after the earth is given over to His enemies and then Christ returns to recapture the earth).

## Simple vs. Difficult Passages

I'm overgeneralizing here, but I think that Pre-millennialism generally struggles to explain simple passages about the end times while explaining the difficult ones well. Partial Preterism can struggle the opposite way — simple passages can be easily explained while difficult ones remain difficult to understand.

One example is Matthew 24:34. A Partial Preterist would take this at face value. The generation Jesus was talking to did not pass away. Simple as that. Pre-millennialists have to jump through some hoops to either choose a less probable definition of "generation" or redefine what it means for Christ's kingdom to come.

Revelation 3:7-13 is another good example. This passage was written to a real first-century church in Philadelphia, letting its members know they would be kept "from the hour of testing, that which is about to come upon the whole world". Jesus goes on to say: "I am coming quickly; hold fast what you have so that no one will take your crown". It remains for another post to examine what Christ means by "coming" in this passage, but it's clear that Christ is setting an expectation of an "hour of testing" and a "coming" in close temporal proximity to the original audience. If this were not so, it wouldn't make sense to say "I am coming quickly" and encourage the church in Philadelphia to persevere on the grounds of this quick coming.

There are still things to explain from a Partial Preterist perspective (e.g., what Christ means by "coming" in Revelation 3:11 and what all of the symbolic imagery means throughout the rest of the book), but it's clear (at least to me) that the letters to the seven churches aren't describing yet-unfulfilled events to spiritual churches. They are real letters written to real churches that actually received them.
